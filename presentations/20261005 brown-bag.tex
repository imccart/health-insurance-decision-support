% ── Motivation ──────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Motivation}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{Intermediaries help consumers, but their incentives may not align with consumers'}
  Home buyers use realtors, investors use financial advisors, insurance buyers use agents.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Intermediaries reduce the cost of a complex decision, but are often paid in a way that depends on which option the consumer picks.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  When commissions vary by option, the intermediary's advice is no longer necessarily disinterested.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Health insurance agents are paid commissions that vary by plan}
  In Medicare Advantage: 43.7\% of new enrollees and 69.8\% of existing policyholders used an agent in 2022, generating \$10.1 billion in commissions.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  In the ACA exchanges: 78\% of active enrollments were agent-facilitated in 2024.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Question: does assistance help consumers choose better, and does commission-based steering undo that benefit?
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{What we do}
  Consumer-level enrollment data from the California ACA exchange, 2014--2019.

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Design-based estimate of how decision assistance affects plan choice (dominated-plan selection)
    \item Structural model of household choice + insurer pricing and commissions
    \item Counterfactuals: remove assistance, reshape commissions, replace agents with navigators
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ── Data and Setting ────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Data and Setting}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{The California ACA exchange}
  Metal tiers (bronze/silver/gold/platinum), standardized cost-sharing within a tier.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Premium and cost-sharing subsidies tied to income; silver plans especially generous for low-income enrollees.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Two channels of decision assistance: public navigators (uncompensated) and private agents (commissioned).
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Data}
  Nearly 8 million household-year observations, 2014--2019, including years in which eligible households were uninsured.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Each household's full choice set, premiums, subsidies, and assistance channel (if any) are observed.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The dominated-plan test}
  For cost-sharing-subsidy-eligible households, silver can be cheaper \emph{and} more generous than gold or platinum.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Choosing gold or platinum over silver in that case is a dominated choice -- a clean, model-free marker of a mistake.
\end{frame}

% ── Decision Assistance and Plan Choice ─────────────────────
\section{Does Assistance Improve Choices?}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{Design: predict the unassisted choice, compare to the assisted one}
  Inverse-propensity-weighted conditional logit estimated on unassisted households.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Use it to predict what assisted households would have chosen absent assistance -- an ATT on the treated.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Assistance cuts dominated-plan selection by 42\%}
  Overall: 42\% reduction (1.6 percentage points).

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  Public navigators: 25\% reduction. Private agents: 48\% reduction.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Private agents move the needle more than public navigators on this metric.
\end{frame}

% ── Structural Model ────────────────────────────────────────
\section{A Model of Choice and Pricing}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{Households choose plans, possibly with help; insurers set premiums and commissions}
  Households: choice among plans, with or without navigator/agent assistance.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Insurers: set premiums and agent commissions, facing risk adjustment and subsidy rules.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Commissions steer enrollment}
  A \$1 increase in monthly commission shifts agent-assisted enrollment as much as a \$1.47 reduction in net monthly premium.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  In elasticity terms, a 1\% change in commissions moves agent-assisted demand roughly 0.22 as much as a 1\% change in premiums.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Why this matters}
  The model lets us separate three things that move together in the data:

  \vspace{0.2cm}
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item the value assistance provides (better-matched plans)
    \item the steering commissions introduce (worse-matched plans)
    \item the premium pass-through of paying for commissions at all
  \end{enumerate}
\end{frame}

% ── Welfare and Counterfactuals ──────────────────────────────
\section{What Would Change Without Agents?}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{Counterfactual 1: withdraw decision assistance entirely}
  Uninsured share rises by 6.1 percentage points.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Welfare falls, driven mainly by lost coverage rather than worse plan matches among those who stay enrolled.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Counterfactual 2: reshape the commission schedule, keep assistance}
  Welfare changes by less than \$50 per member per year.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Coverage barely moves. Commission \emph{design} is a second-order lever relative to whether assistance exists at all.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Counterfactual 3: replace private agents with public navigators}
  Coverage essentially unchanged.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Premiums fall because the commission costs insurers pass through to \emph{all} enrollees disappear.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Welfare rises.
\end{frame}

% ── Conclusion ───────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}
\sectionframe

\begin{frame}{Decision assistance is worth having; who pays for it matters less than whether it exists}
  Assistance improves plan choice; losing it costs coverage, not just match quality.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Commission-based steering is real but small relative to the value of assistance itself.

  \vspace{0.3cm}
  Policy relevance: federal navigator funding has been cut, restored, and cut again; MA has tried to cap agent commissions on steering grounds.
\end{frame}
